\textbf{How many copies?} With log-probabilities and no cleaning (Fig.~\ref{fig:dose}, \emph{none}),
\pythia{160M} detects watermarks from a single copy (AUC 0.67), random sequences and fluent traps from 4
(AUC 0.64--0.65), and fictional facts from 16 (AUC 0.66). Model size matters a lot. \pythia{410M} detects every
type from a single copy: random sequences and watermarks at AUC 0.90, enough for a 10-canary bundle
(Table~\ref{tab:bundle}). Fictional facts are the weakest at one copy (AUC 0.61, all 200 needed) but reach AUC 0.83
at 16 copies, where 25 facts suffice. Detection rises steadily with dose at both sizes (App.~\ref{app:all}).

\textbf{Single copies only stand out among repeated look-alikes.} The substring-dedup corpus
contains exactly the same 1-copy canaries as \emph{none}: all 200 random sequences planted once survive intact
(App.~\ref{app:survival}). What it lacks are the repeated copies of the \emph{other} canaries of the same type.
Yet on \pythia{410M}, 1-copy random sequences fall from AUC 0.90 (\emph{none}) to 0.54 (dedup), fluent traps from
0.74 to 0.54, and watermarks from 0.90 to 0.67. Fictional facts, which dedup leaves alone, stay at 0.61. Our
reading (exploratory): once a model has learned a canary \emph{format} from many repeated examples, one more
example of that format is memorized far more easily. A writer who plants only single copies, with no repeated
canaries of the same kind in the corpus, should expect the dedup numbers, not the \emph{none} numbers.

\textbf{Does cleaning remove them?} Each verbatim canary type has one pipeline that defeats it, at both model
sizes (Fig.~\ref{fig:survival}). Substring deduplication keeps only the first copy of every repeated 50-token span,
so 4.5--4.7\% of verbatim copies survive (one per canary). After it, random sequences and fluent traps are not
detectable at any dose, even on \pythia{410M}. Watermarks keep a weak signal there (AUC 0.64--0.67, 100 canaries
needed). NFKC normalization removes every zero-width character, but the carrier sentence remains, so watermarks
degrade into weaker fluent-text canaries (16-copy TPR 0.98$\to$0.12 at 160M, 1.00$\to$0.62 at 410M).
Paraphrasing 30\% of copies weakens verbatim types at 160M (16-copy TPR of random sequences 0.46$\to$0.17), but
\pythia{410M} still detects them from one copy. Fictional facts are the only type that no pipeline affects: each copy
is already phrased differently, so dedup finds nothing to remove and paraphrasing changes nothing that matters.

\textbf{Is text-only access enough?} It is for verbatim canaries on the larger model, but never for facts
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dose}, right). On \pythia{160M}, greedy completion recovers watermarks from 16 copies and fluent
traps only at 64. On \pythia{410M}, all three verbatim types are recovered from 4 copies, with 16-copy TPRs of
0.29--0.67 (Fig.~\ref{fig:survival}). Neither dedup nor, at 160M, normalization leaves enough for text-only detection.
Fictional facts are not reliably detected from sampled answers to a held-out question at either size (AUC $\le
0.55$ in every cell, and only scattered borderline cells reach $p<0.01$ on \pythia{410M}). The gray-box version of the
same question (log-probability of the answer, a secondary detector) reaches only AUC 0.65 at 64 copies on
\pythia{410M}, while the loss on a planted phrasing reaches 0.98. The models memorize the sentences they saw much
more than they learn the fact.

\textbf{Can we avoid false accusations?} Yes. On both untouched base models, none of the 32 primary cells reaches
$p<0.01$. Across 200 random re-splits per cell, 1.1\% (160M) and 1.0\% (410M) of p-values fall below 0.01, and
they follow the uniform distribution (Table~\ref{tab:bundle}, App.~\ref{app:qq}). The false-positive rate is
controlled at the nominal level, as the randomized split guarantees.
