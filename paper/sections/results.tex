\textbf{How many copies?} With log-probabilities and no cleaning (Fig.~\ref{fig:dose}, left, \emph{none}),
invisible-character watermarks are the most copy-efficient canary: detectable from a single copy
(AUC 0.67), and at 4 copies a bundle of only 10 watermarked canaries suffices (Table~\ref{tab:bundle}). Random
sequences and fluent traps are detectable from 4 copies (AUC 0.64--0.65), but at that dose a writer needs about
100 of them; at 16 copies, 10 are enough. Fictional facts need 16 copies (AUC 0.66, 100 canaries), and 64 copies
to work with a 10-canary bundle. All detection rates rise steadily with dose (full table in
App.~\ref{app:all}).

\textbf{Does cleaning remove them?} Each verbatim canary type has one pipeline that defeats it
(Fig.~\ref{fig:survival}, App.~\ref{app:survival}). Substring deduplication keeps only the first copy of every
repeated 50-token span, so 4.5--4.7\% of verbatim copies survive (one per canary). After it, random
sequences, fluent traps and watermarks are not reliably detectable at any dose: the only significant cell is an
isolated 1-copy watermark hit that no higher dose reproduces. NFKC normalization removes every zero-width
character, but the carrier sentence remains, so watermarks degrade into weaker fluent-text canaries (TPR at
16 copies falls from 0.98 to 0.12; detectable from 4 copies instead of 1). Paraphrasing 30\% of copies halves the
16-copy TPR of random sequences and fluent traps (0.46$\to$0.17, 0.53$\to$0.20) but leaves them detectable from
4 copies. Fictional facts are the only type that no pipeline affects: each copy is already phrased differently,
so deduplication finds nothing to remove and paraphrasing changes nothing that matters (detectable from 16 copies
under every pipeline).

\textbf{Is text-only access enough?} Mostly not, at the doses we test (Fig.~\ref{fig:dose}, right). Greedy
completion recovers watermarked suffixes from 16 copies (AUC 0.62) and fluent traps only at 64 (AUC 0.73).
Random sequences show one isolated 1-copy hit ($p=0.008$) that no higher dose reproduces, so we do not count it.
Fictional facts are never detected from sampled answers to a held-out question (AUC $\le 0.52$ in every cell). The
gray-box version of the same question (log-probability of the answer, a secondary detector) reaches only AUC
0.63 at 64 copies, while the loss on a planted phrasing reaches 0.88. At this scale the model memorizes the
sentences it saw rather than learning the fact.

\textbf{Can we avoid false accusations?} Yes. On the untouched base model, none of the 32 primary cells reaches
$p<0.01$. Across 200 random re-splits per cell, 1.1\% of p-values fall below 0.01 and 4.9\% below 0.05, and they
follow the uniform distribution (Fig.~\ref{fig:qq}; Kolmogorov--Smirnov $p=0.08$ for the gray-box detector). The
false-positive rate is controlled at the nominal level, as the randomized split guarantees.
